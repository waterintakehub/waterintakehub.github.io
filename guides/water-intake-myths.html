Water Intake Myths Debunked

The most common wrong ideas about drinking water, from eight glasses to coffee, and what is actually true.

Water advice is full of confident rules, and many of them are wrong or misunderstood. Some come from marketing, some from old health advice, and some from a misunderstanding of real science. Believing them can lead to drinking too little, drinking too much, or worrying unnecessarily. This guide takes the most common myths and sets them against what is actually known.

Myth: you must drink eight glasses a day

Eight glasses, about 2 litres, is a reasonable average for an average adult, but it is not a rule that applies to everyone. A small, sedentary person needs less, and a large, active person in the heat needs more. The better approach is to calculate from your weight, about 30 to 35 ml per kilogram, and adjust for activity and climate. The eight-glasses rule is a starting point, not a prescription, and it includes water from food as well as drinks.

Myth: coffee and tea dehydrate you

Coffee and tea do have a mild diuretic effect, but the water in the drink more than compensates for it at normal intakes. A cup of tea or coffee contributes almost as much fluid as a cup of water. The myth comes from studies of large caffeine doses, not from normal drinking. Two to four cups a day is fine for most healthy adults, and they count towards your daily fluid total.

Myth: you should drink as much as possible

More water is not always better. Drinking far more than you need, especially in a short time, can dilute blood sodium and cause hyponatraemia, which is dangerous. The body has a good system for regulating water, and thirst is a reasonable guide for most people. Drink to a sensible target spread through the day, and let pale urine be your guide, not a maximum volume.

Myth: thirst means you are already dehydrated

Thirst is an early signal, not a sign of failure. It appears when the body needs water, and drinking when you feel thirsty is usually enough to stay hydrated. The idea that thirst means you are already dangerously dehydrated is exaggerated. Thirst becomes less reliable with age and during exercise, which is why a schedule helps in those cases, but for most healthy adults, drinking when thirsty works well.

Myth: clear urine means perfect hydration

Pale straw-coloured urine is the sign of good hydration, not completely clear urine. If your urine is clear all day and you are going to the toilet very often, you may be drinking more than you need. Dark yellow urine means you need more. The colour is a useful guide, but it is affected by vitamins, medicines and foods such as beetroot, so use it alongside thirst and how you feel, not as a sole measure.

Testing the myths yourself

Most water myths can be tested in a week. Think you must drink eight glasses? Calculate from your weight and compare: the number is often different, and your urine colour will tell you which is right. Believe coffee dehydrates you? Drink your usual coffee and check your urine colour and thirst: both will be normal, because the water in the drink outweighs the diuretic effect. Think clear urine is the goal? Look at the colour over a week: pale straw is the sign of good hydration, and completely clear all day means you are drinking more than you need. Personal observation beats confident rules, and a week of attention settles most of the myths for good. Keep the habit of checking, because new myths appear every year, and the same test still works.

Frequently Asked Questions

Must everyone drink eight glasses a day?

No. It is a rough average. Calculate from your weight, about 30 to 35 ml per kilogram, and adjust for activity and climate.

Do coffee and tea dehydrate you?

No, not at normal intakes. They count towards fluid intake almost as much as water.

Is clear urine the goal?

No. Pale straw is the goal. Completely clear all day can mean you are drinking more than needed.

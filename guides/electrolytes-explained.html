Electrolytes Explained

What electrolytes are, why sweat depletes them, when you need to replace them, and how food and drinks do it.

Electrolytes are minerals, mainly sodium, potassium, calcium and magnesium, that carry an electric charge and help control fluid balance, muscle function and nerve signals. You lose them in sweat, and you replace them through food and drink. For most people, ordinary meals cover the loss. For long, sweaty exercise, replacement matters more. This guide explains what electrolytes do and when to think about them.

What electrolytes do

Sodium is the main electrolyte lost in sweat, and it helps the body hold on to water and maintain blood volume. Potassium, found in fruit, vegetables and dairy, helps muscles and nerves work. Calcium and magnesium are involved in muscle contraction and bone health. Together, they keep the fluid inside and outside your cells in balance. When electrolytes fall too low, the symptoms include cramps, tiredness, dizziness and confusion, though this is uncommon in everyday life.

How much do you lose in sweat

Sweat is mostly water, but it contains sodium at roughly 0.5 to 1 gram per litre, plus smaller amounts of potassium, calcium and magnesium. An hour of hard exercise in heat might cost a litre of sweat and close to a gram of sodium. That sounds like a lot, but a normal diet provides several grams of sodium a day, so a single session rarely causes a problem. It is repeated long sessions, especially over several days, that can deplete sodium if it is not replaced.

When replacement matters

For exercise under about 90 minutes, water and a normal meal afterwards are enough. For longer sessions, or for work in extreme heat over a full day, replacing salts matters: a sports drink, or water plus a salty snack, does the job. The sign that salts are needed is often a craving for salty food after a long session, or cramps that keep returning. Do not take salt tablets without advice, because the dose matters and too much salt carries its own risks.

Food as an electrolyte source

Ordinary food is the best source of electrolytes. Bananas, potatoes, yoghurt and dried fruit are rich in potassium. Dairy, leafy greens and fortified foods provide calcium. Nuts, seeds and whole grains provide magnesium. Sodium comes from salt in cooking and in foods such as bread, cheese and soup. A balanced meal after exercise replaces the electrolytes of a normal session without any special products.

Sports drinks: useful or not

Sports drinks contain water, a small amount of carbohydrate, and electrolytes, mainly sodium and potassium. They are useful for long sessions, where the carbohydrate provides energy and the salts replace losses. For short sessions, they are unnecessary: water is enough, and the sugar adds calories without benefit. If you use them, choose one with a modest sugar content, or make your own by adding a pinch of salt and a little fruit juice to water. For most people, most of the time, water and food are the better choice.

A long training day, planned

Take a cyclist riding for three hours in summer heat, losing about a litre of sweat an hour. The total loss is about 3 litres of water and 2 to 3 grams of sodium. Water alone would replace the fluid but not the salts, and drinking 3 litres of plain water could dilute blood sodium. The plan is to drink about 500 to 800 ml per hour, using a sports drink or water plus salty snacks, and to eat a salty meal afterwards. The sports drink provides carbohydrate for energy and sodium for retention, and the meal replaces potassium, calcium and magnesium. For a one-hour ride, the same plan is unnecessary: water and a normal meal are enough. The difference is duration, and knowing where the line falls is the practical skill.

Tip: For exercise under about 90 minutes, water and a normal meal are enough. Salts matter for longer sessions, where a sports drink or a salty snack replaces what sweat takes.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are electrolytes?

Minerals such as sodium, potassium, calcium and magnesium that control fluid balance, muscle function and nerve signals.

Do I need electrolyte drinks?

Usually not. For sessions under 90 minutes, water and food are enough. Longer or very hot sessions benefit from salts.

What foods replace electrolytes?

Bananas and potatoes for potassium, dairy and greens for calcium, nuts and grains for magnesium, and salt in food for sodium.

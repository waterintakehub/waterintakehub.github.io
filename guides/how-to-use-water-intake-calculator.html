How to Use a Water Intake Calculator

A step-by-step guide to getting a realistic daily water target from your weight, activity and climate.

A water intake calculator turns your body weight and your daily life into a number: the litres of water you should aim to drink each day. The maths is simple, but the answer is only useful if you enter honest numbers and understand what the result does and does not include. This guide walks through the process step by step.

Start with your weight in kilograms

The calculator's base figure comes from your weight, using the common guideline of about 30 to 35 ml of water per kilogram of body weight per day. A 70 kg person therefore starts at about 2,100 to 2,450 ml before any adjustments. Weigh yourself in the morning, before eating, and use that number. If you only know your weight in pounds, divide by 2.2 to get kilograms. A weight that is 10 kg out shifts the result by about 350 ml, so a recent, accurate weight is worth the minute it takes.

Add activity honestly

Exercise raises water needs because you lose water as sweat. A moderate workout of an hour can cost 500 ml or more, and a hard session in heat can cost over a litre an hour. The calculator adds a fixed amount for your general activity level, but if you train hard on a particular day, add more for that day using the activity calculator. A desk day and a training day can differ by a litre or more, and a single daily target will be wrong for one of them. Consider keeping two targets: a rest-day target and a training-day target.

Add climate on top

Heat and humidity raise sweat losses even without exercise. Above about 30 degrees Celsius, most people need several hundred millilitres more a day, and above 35 degrees the extra can pass a litre. Humidity matters because sweat evaporates less well in humid air, so the body sweats more to achieve the same cooling. If you spend hours outdoors in heat, add more still. Our climate calculator works this out for your temperature and outdoor time.

Understand what the number includes

The target is total water intake, and about a fifth of it usually comes from food: fruit, vegetables, soups, yoghurt and cooked grains all contain water. So a target of 2,500 ml does not mean drinking 2,500 ml of water; it means about 2,000 ml of drinks plus the water in food. Tea, coffee and milk also count towards fluid intake, despite old claims that they do not. What does not count is alcohol, which increases water loss rather than replacing it.

Turn the number into a habit

A target only helps if you can follow it. Divide the day into drinks: a glass on waking, one with each meal, one mid-morning, one mid-afternoon, and one in the evening, with a bottle at your desk or in your bag. Use the hydration schedule planner to spread the target across your waking hours. Check your urine colour as a rough guide: pale straw is about right, dark yellow means drink more, and completely clear all day can mean you are drinking more than you need. Adjust the target over a week or two until the habit feels natural.

A worked example from start to finish

Take a 75 kg person who exercises moderately and lives in a temperate climate. The base is 75 times 35, or 2,625 ml. Activity adds 350 ml and climate adds nothing, giving 2,975 ml, or about 3 litres a day. About a fifth comes from food, so the amount to drink is about 2,400 ml, or roughly ten glasses of 250 ml. On a training day in summer, the same person might need 3,500 ml in total, or about 2,800 ml to drink. The example shows why a single fixed target is misleading: the number changes with the day, and the calculator is most useful when it is run for a rest day and a training day separately. Keep the two targets on the fridge, and the habit follows the day rather than fighting it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water should I drink a day?

A common guideline is about 30 to 35 ml per kilogram of body weight, plus more for exercise and heat. For a 70 kg adult that is roughly 2.1 to 2.5 litres, plus food water.

Does food count towards water intake?

Yes. About a fifth of daily water usually comes from food, especially fruit, vegetables, soups and yoghurt.

Do tea and coffee count?

Yes, they count towards fluid intake. Alcohol does not, because it increases water loss.

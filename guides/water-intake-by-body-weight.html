Water Intake by Body Weight

How the 30 to 35 ml per kilogram guideline works, with worked examples for different weights.

The simplest way to estimate daily water needs is from body weight: about 30 to 35 ml of water per kilogram per day. It is a guideline used by dietitians and health services because it scales with the body it is meant to serve. This guide explains the maths, works through examples for different weights, and shows when to use the lower or upper end of the range.

The formula

Multiply your weight in kilograms by 30 for the lower end of the range, and by 35 for the upper end. The result is your estimated total daily water intake in millilitres, including the water in food. A 70 kg person therefore needs roughly 2,100 to 2,450 ml a day. The range exists because needs vary: a sedentary person in a cool climate sits near the lower end, while an active person in warmth sits near the upper end. Our calculator uses 35 ml per kilogram as the base and then adds for activity and climate.

Worked examples

A 50 kg person needs about 1,500 to 1,750 ml a day. A 60 kg person needs about 1,800 to 2,100 ml. A 70 kg person needs about 2,100 to 2,450 ml. An 80 kg person needs about 2,400 to 2,800 ml. A 90 kg person needs about 2,700 to 3,150 ml. A 100 kg person needs about 3,000 to 3,500 ml. Remember that about a fifth of this total comes from food, so the amount to drink is roughly 80 percent of the figure: a 70 kg person needs to drink about 1,700 to 2,000 ml, or seven to eight glasses of 250 ml.

When to use the lower end

Use the lower end of the range, around 30 ml per kilogram, if you are mostly sedentary, live in a cool climate, spend most of the day indoors, and eat plenty of water-rich foods such as fruit, vegetables and soups. Older adults and people with certain health conditions may also need less, and a doctor can advise on the right figure. If your urine is pale and you feel well, the lower end is enough, and there is no benefit in forcing more.

When to use the upper end

Use the upper end, around 35 ml per kilogram or more, if you are active, work outdoors, live in a hot climate, or sweat heavily. Add more on top for exercise: an hour of moderate training can add 500 ml or more to the day's needs. Pregnancy and breastfeeding also raise needs. If your urine is dark, you feel thirsty often, or you get headaches in the afternoon, try the upper end for a week and see whether the signs improve.

Weight changes mean the target changes

Because the formula is based on weight, the target moves when your weight moves. Losing 10 kg reduces the base need by about 300 to 350 ml a day, and gaining 10 kg raises it by the same amount. Recalculate whenever your weight changes by 5 kg or more, or every few months. It is a small detail, but it keeps the target honest, and it is one of the reasons a fixed eight-glasses rule becomes wrong over time.

A table you can keep

Write your own table and keep it on the fridge. For a 50 kg person, 1,500 to 1,750 ml. For 60 kg, 1,800 to 2,100 ml. For 70 kg, 2,100 to 2,450 ml. For 80 kg, 2,400 to 2,800 ml. For 90 kg, 2,700 to 3,150 ml. For 100 kg, 3,000 to 3,500 ml. These are total water figures, including food, so the amount to drink is roughly 80 percent of each number. Add 500 ml for a hot day or a hard training session, and subtract nothing for a rest day in cool weather. Recalculate the table whenever your weight changes by 5 kg, and the target stays honest. A table on the fridge beats a rule in your head, because it is specific to you and visible every day.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water per kilogram of body weight?

About 30 to 35 ml per kilogram per day, including water from food. A 70 kg person needs roughly 2,100 to 2,450 ml.

Does the target change if I lose weight?

Yes. Losing 10 kg cuts the base need by about 300 to 350 ml a day, so recalculate after a 5 kg change.

Should I use 30 or 35 ml per kilogram?

Use 30 if you are sedentary in a cool climate, 35 if you are active or in warmth. Adjust from your urine colour and thirst.

Signs of Dehydration to Watch For

The early and later signs that you are not drinking enough, who is most at risk, and what to do about it.

Dehydration is the loss of more water than you take in, and it can develop quietly over a day of forgetting to drink. The early signs are easy to miss or to blame on something else: tiredness, a headache, a dry mouth. Knowing the signs means you can act early, when a glass of water is all it takes. This guide explains what to look for, who is most at risk, and when dehydration needs medical attention.

Early signs

The earliest signs are thirst, a dry mouth, and urine that is dark yellow with a strong smell. You may also notice tiredness, a mild headache, dizziness when standing up, and difficulty concentrating. Your skin may feel dry, and you may go to the toilet less often than usual. These signs mean your body is already short of water, and the fix is simple: drink a glass or two of water now, and keep drinking regularly for the rest of the day. Most mild dehydration improves within an hour or two of drinking.

Later signs

If dehydration continues, the signs get stronger: a very dry mouth and tongue, sunken eyes, rapid heartbeat, low blood pressure, confusion, and very little urine, which may be dark amber. Skin loses its elasticity: pinch the skin on the back of your hand and it stays tented for a moment instead of springing back. Muscle cramps can appear, especially after exercise in heat. These signs need more than a glass of water: drink steadily, rest in a cool place, and seek medical advice if they do not improve.

Who is most at risk

Children are at higher risk because they have a larger surface area relative to their weight and may not recognise thirst. Older adults are at risk because their sense of thirst weakens with age and their kidneys conserve water less well. People who work outdoors, exercise hard, or live in hot climates lose more water. Illness raises the risk too: fever, vomiting and diarrhoea all increase water loss, and anyone who cannot keep fluids down should seek medical advice promptly.

What to do about mild dehydration

For mild dehydration, drink water steadily, in small amounts, rather than a large volume at once. Oral rehydration solutions, available from pharmacies, replace water and salts together and are particularly useful after vomiting, diarrhoea, or heavy sweating. Eat water-rich foods such as fruit and soup, rest, and avoid alcohol and caffeine until you feel better. Keep drinking for the rest of the day, because replacing a deficit takes hours, not minutes.

When to get medical help

Seek medical help if you cannot keep fluids down, if you have not urinated for eight hours or more, if you feel confused or very drowsy, if your heart is racing, or if a child is unusually sleepy, has a dry mouth, or has had no wet nappy for several hours. Severe dehydration needs medical treatment with fluids, and it can develop quickly in children and older adults. Do not wait for it to pass on its own if the signs are strong or getting worse.

A quick self-check you can do anywhere

Check yourself in thirty seconds. First, look at your urine colour: pale straw is good, dark yellow means drink now. Second, pinch the skin on the back of your hand: if it springs back immediately, you are probably fine, and if it stays tented for a moment, you may be short of water. Third, ask yourself three questions: am I thirsty, do I have a headache, and do I feel unusually tired? Two yes answers mean drink a glass now and keep drinking for the rest of the day. The check is rough, not a diagnosis, but it catches mild dehydration early, when a glass of water is all it takes. Do it at midday and in the late afternoon, the two times people most often forget to drink.

Tip: Dark yellow urine, a dry mouth, tiredness and headache are the early signs — and most mild dehydration improves within an hour or two of drinking steadily.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the first signs of dehydration?

Thirst, dry mouth, dark yellow urine, tiredness, mild headache and going to the toilet less often.

Who is most at risk of dehydration?

Children, older adults, outdoor workers, people exercising in heat, and anyone with fever, vomiting or diarrhoea.

When is dehydration an emergency?

If you cannot keep fluids down, have not urinated for eight hours, feel confused or very drowsy, or a child is unusually sleepy — seek medical help.
